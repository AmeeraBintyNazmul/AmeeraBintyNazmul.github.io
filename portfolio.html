MSS THESIS Jan – Jul 2026

Foreign Finance and Governance Impact on the Renewable Energy–Growth–Environment Nexus: A Comparison Analysis of SAARC and ASEAN

This study estimates the disaggregated effects of official development assistance, foreign direct investment, and personal remittances on renewable energy consumption, and assesses whether government effectiveness and political stability moderate these channels. It further extends the analysis to environmental outcomes using PM2.5 mean values. Using a balanced annual panel of six ASEAN and five SAARC economies over 1996–2024, and accounting for cross-sectional dependence and slope heterogeneity, the study applies cross-sectionally augmented ARDL and distributed-lag estimators, Driscoll–Kraay regressions, Dumitrescu–Hurlin causality tests, and a pooled bloc-difference test.

Seventeen of twenty-four channels form stable long-run relationships. In ASEAN, the long run is income-led, with no independent effect of foreign finance identified. In SAARC, government effectiveness shows a robust negative association with renewable energy consumption across all estimators. The two blocs differ significantly in their long-run relationships while remaining indistinguishable in the short run, and growth and renewable energy are causally neutral. Government effectiveness is associated with lower fine-particulate exposure in ASEAN, though this link is not stable, and renewable consumption shows no direct air-quality effect in either region. These findings suggest that raising the volume of foreign finance alone is unlikely to advance the renewable transition; country-specific, governance-sensitive strategies are needed.

BSS PROJECT Jan – Jul 2025

Eating Behavior & Food Choice Among Public University Students

This study qualitatively explores the changes and factors influencing food choices among students living away from home at four public universities in Dhaka. Using purposive sampling, 21 students were recruited and data were collected through 14 IDIs and 2 FGDs. Thematic analysis identified four broad categories — individual, social, physical, and university-related factors — determining students' food decisions.

Dietary shifts included reduced intake of fish, vegetables, and fruit, higher junk food consumption, and irregular meal patterns, driven by inaccessibility of nutritious meals, monotonous taste, limited healthy alternatives, academic pressures, and inadequate cooking facilities. Notably, healthy food was perceived as unavailable and inaccessible rather than unaffordable. This is the first study to highlight the influence of menstrual cycle, health shocks, and homesickness on food decisions of students living independently in Dhaka.
